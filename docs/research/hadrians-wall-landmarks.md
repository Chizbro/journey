# Hadrian's Wall Path: Landmarks, distances, and factual traps

**Date of access: 2026-08-08.** Sources are, in order of preference: the official National Trail
site (`nationaltrail.co.uk`, Natural England), English Heritage, the National Trust, Historic
England (including National Heritage List entries), Northumberland National Park Authority / The
Sill, North East Museums (Tyne & Wear), the Vindolanda Trust, Roman Inscriptions of Britain
(`romaninscriptionsofbritain.org`), the Crown Prosecution Service, and published archaeology.
Wikipedia was used only to locate leads. Every claim below carries a URL. Where I could not source
something authoritatively I have said so rather than guessed — see **Open questions / unverified**.

---

## Summary: what to build

**20 Landmarks, spanning 0 km (Segedunum, Wallsend) to 135 km (Bowness-on-Solway).** The route is
84 miles / 135 km, Wallsend in the east to Bowness-on-Solway in the west
([National Trails](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/)).

**Median gap between Landmarks is about 6 km; the distribution is genuinely lumpy and cannot be
fixed without inventing things.** Two dead stretches:

| Dead stretch | Length | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heddon-on-the-Wall (24 km) → Halton Chesters (40 km)** | **16 km** | The Military Road (B6318, built 1751–57) was driven along the Wall's foundations here. There is almost no upstanding masonry for 15 miles. |
| **Walton (95 km) → Carlisle (112 km)** | **17 km** | The Wall west of the Irthing was first built in turf, and what stone there was has been robbed out. The official route description for this stage opens "By now the lack of masonry won't concern you" ([National Trails route description](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/route/)). |

Against that, the central sector (62–89 km) has eight Landmarks in 27 km. **That asymmetry is the
truth of the route** — the Whin Sill crags are where everything is. Optional fillers for the two
gaps, and optional trims for the crowded middle, are listed in §5.

**Distances are derived, not quoted.** The National Trail site publishes the total (84 mi / 135 km)
and one internal figure (Chollerford→Birdoswald, 23 mi / 37 km) but **no cumulative distance
chart**. See §1 for the derivation and the error bars. Treat every figure as ±2 km, and the eastern
sector as ±3 km.

**Three traps a copywriter will otherwise fall into** (full list in §4):

1. **The Sycamore Gap tree is a fenced, living stump with coppice shoots.** Felled 27–28 September
   2023; two men jailed 4 years 3 months each on 15 July 2025. The largest surviving trunk section
   is indoors at The Sill, not at the gap. It was also **not 300 years old** — Historic England's
   ring count says 100–120 years minimum, planted late 19th century.
2. **Vindolanda is not on the Wall and is not on the Path.** It is ~1.5 km south, on the Stanegate,
   and its first fort predates the Wall by about 40 years. It is a separate charity with separate
   tickets from the English Heritage sites. Include it — but as an explicit detour.
3. **The Wall is not, and never was, the England–Scotland border**, and "defensive rampart" is the
   wrong frame. Hadrian's Wall Country, the World Heritage Site partnership, states plainly: *"It's
   sometimes mistakenly thought that the Wall marks the English/Scottish border. In fact,
   Northumberland, England's largest county, lies mainly north of Hadrian's Wall"* and *"a function,
   probably the chief function, of the Wall, was frontier control, just like modern frontier
   barriers"* ([Hadrian's Wall Country](https://hadrianswallcountry.co.uk/explore/the-history-of-the-wall/)).

## 1. How the distances were derived, and how much to trust them

### What is actually published

- **Total: 84 miles (135 km)**, Wallsend → Bowness-on-Solway
  ([National Trails](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/)).
- **Chollerford → Birdoswald: 23 miles (37 km)** — the only internal distance the official site
  gives, quoted as the hardest section
  ([National Trails, Trail Information](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/trail-information/)).
- The official **Route Description** divides the trail into six named sections but **publishes no
  distance for any of them**
  ([National Trails route description](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/route/)).

### The six-section anchors

Wikipedia's article carries per-section mileages matching the official six sections exactly. I
checked the wikitext: **those per-section figures are uncited** — only the 84-mile total carries a
citation, and it cites the National Trail site
([Wikipedia wikitext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Export/Hadrian%27s_Wall_Path)). So they
are *consistent with* the official sectioning but are not themselves authoritative. They are the
best available and they survive independent cross-checks (below), so I use them as anchors and say
so plainly.

| Section | Section distance | Cumulative from Wallsend |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wallsend → Heddon-on-the-Wall | 15 mi / 24 km | **24 km** |
| Heddon-on-the-Wall → Chollerford | 15.5 mi / 25 km | **49 km** |
| Chollerford → Steel Rigg | 12 mi / 19 km | **68 km** |
| Steel Rigg → Walton | 16.25 mi / 26 km | **95 km** |
| Walton → Carlisle | 11 mi / 18 km | **112 km** |
| Carlisle → Bowness-on-Solway | 14.75 mi / 24 km | **136 km** |

Sum: 84.5 mi / 136 km, against an official 84 mi / 135 km. **I use 135 km as the Journey total** and
present Bowness at 135; the 1 km is rounding in the section figures.

### Cross-checks that passed

- **Chollerford → Birdoswald.** Anchors give Chollerford at 49 km; the official 37 km puts
  Birdoswald at **86 km**. My interpolation (below) puts it at **84.6 km**. Agreement to 1.5 km.
- **Newcastle Quayside → Wallsend** measures 4.9 mi / 7.9 km on recorded GPS tracks
  ([AllTrails](https://www.alltrails.com/trail/england/tyne-and-wear/hadrian-s-wall-west-to-east-newcastle-quayside-to-wallsend)),
  and Heddon → Newcastle Quayside is ~16 km, together ~24 km. That matches the 15-mile section
  figure, and rules out the inflated 16.8-mile figure some tour operators quote (they measure to
  accommodation, not to the trail point).

### Interpolation between anchors

Within each section I place Landmarks by **Wall mile** — the Roman milecastle numbering, 1–80 west
from Wallsend at one Roman mile (≈1,480 m) intervals, which is a real published linear reference
system for the frontier
([English Heritage, Sewingshields](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/sewingshields-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
A Landmark's Wall mile is converted to path kilometres by linear interpolation between the two
bracketing anchors. This works because the Path shadows the Wall closely everywhere except the
Tyneside section.

**Systematic caveat, stated up front:** the Path is *not* the Wall. Between Wallsend and
Heddon-on-the-Wall the National Trail deliberately takes the riverside instead of the Wall's line
through Newcastle's suburbs — *"a riverside route for the 12 miles between the eastern end of the
Wall at Wallsend and Heddon-on-the-Wall"*
([North East Museums](https://www.northeastmuseums.org.uk/walking-hadrian-s-wall-on-tyneside)). In
that section I used measured riverside distances, not Wall miles.

### Confidence

| Sector | Confidence | Error bar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchor points (Wallsend, Heddon, Chollerford, Steel Rigg area, Walton, Carlisle, Bowness) | High | ±1 km |
| Central sector interpolations (Chollerford → Walton) | Medium-high | ±1.5 km |
| Eastern riverside (Newcastle, Newburn) | Medium | ±2 km |
| Western sector (Carlisle → Bowness) | Medium | ±2 km |

For an app where a Landmark fires when accumulated distance passes it, ±2 km is immaterial — it
shifts an arrival by less than half a day at 5 km/day. **Do not present these numbers to users as
precise trail mileages.**

## 3. Landmark detail — the raw material for the writing

Each entry gives verifiable specifics. Facts are chosen to be *writable* — a name, a number, an
object, something a person did.

### 1. Segedunum Roman Fort, Wallsend — 0.0 km · **eastern terminus**

*Terminus · Roman fort · museum · North East Museums for North Tyneside Council (admission charged)*

- **The Wall did not originally reach Wallsend.** It first ran only as far east as Pons Aelius
  (Newcastle); Segedunum and a **four-mile eastern extension** were added c. AD 125–127, and the
  extension was built to a **narrower gauge (2.5 m)** than the original 3 m Wall
  ([North East Museums](https://www.northeastmuseums.org.uk/segedunum/about-us),
  [North East Museums](https://www.northeastmuseums.org.uk/segedunum/learning/the-building-and-development-of-hadrian-s-wall)).
- A **branch wall ran from the fort's south-east corner down into the Tyne**, extended into the
  water to low-tide level — the frontier physically ended in the river
  ([Historic UK](https://www.historic-uk.com/HistoryMagazine/DestinationsUK/Wallsend-Segedunum-Roman-Fort/)).
- Built for a *cohors equitata*: **480 infantry and 120 cavalry, 600 men**, on 4 acres. The 3rd- and
  4th-century garrison was **cohors IV Lingonum equitata**, from around modern Langres in eastern
  France, named in the *Notitia Dignitatum*
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/segedunum/),
  [RIB, Wallsend](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/sites/wallsend)).
- **RIB 1300** is an altar to Jupiter set up by **Aelius Rufus, prefect of the Fourth Cohort of
  Lingones** — found in **1783 in the foundations of Tynemouth Priory**, three miles away, where
  someone had carried it as building stone
  ([RIB 1300](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1300)).
- **The fort disappeared under terraced housing in the 1880s and stayed buried for a century.** When
  the houses were demolished, **Charles Daniels of Newcastle University excavated almost the entire
  fort between 1975 and 1984**, making Wallsend *"the most fully investigated of Roman forts in
  Britain"*
  ([Current Archaeology](https://www.archaeology.co.uk/articles/review-segedunum-excavations-charles-daniels-roman-fort-wallsend-1975-1984.htm)).
  The cavalry barracks had **a waste trough in the front chamber for the horses and the riders in
  the back**.
- The museum opened on **17 June 2000** in converted **Swan Hunter shipyard buildings** — the main
  block was built in 1943 as the shipyard canteen
  ([TWAM blog](https://blog.twmuseums.org.uk/recalling-the-opening-of-segedunum-roman-fort-to-the-public-20-years-ago/)).
  There is a **35 m viewing tower**, an **80 m stretch of original Wall standing up to eight courses
  high**, and a full-scale reconstructed Wall section across Buddle Street
  ([National Trails](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/attraction/segedunum-roman-fort-baths-and-museum/)).
- **Status check before writing:** the **reconstructed bath house is currently closed for essential
  maintenance**
  ([Visit Hadrian's Wall](https://www.visithadrianswall.co.uk/things-to-do/segedunum-roman-fort-baths-and-museum-p715271/)).
  A £499,000 grant paid for a new roof, lift and climate control, enabling loans from institutions
  including the British Museum
  ([North East Museums](https://www.northeastmuseums.org.uk/news/segedunum-roman-fort-and-museum-unveils-new-exhibitions-following-major-infrastructure-upgrade)).

### 2. Newcastle Quayside and the site of Pons Aelius — 7.5 km

*City · Roman bridge site*

- ***Pons* means bridge; *Aelius* was Hadrian's own family name** — Publius Aelius Hadrianus. It was
  **the only bridge outside Rome named after an emperor**
  ([Co-Curate, Newcastle University](https://co-curate.ncl.ac.uk/pons-aelius)).
- **The best fact on the whole eastern half.** In **1875**, while men were sinking the foundations
  of the **Swing Bridge**, they pulled two Roman altars out of the north channel of the Tyne. One,
  **RIB 1319**, is dedicated **to Neptune by the Sixth Legion Victrix**, and is carved with **a
  trident entwined by a dolphin**; its pair, **RIB 1320**, is **to Oceanus**, carved with **a ship's
  anchor**. They stood in a shrine on the bridge, at the point where the river god met the god of
  the tide
  ([RIB 1319](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1319),
  [RIB 1320](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1320)).
- The fort's location was argued over until **F. G. Simpson found it by excavation beneath the
  medieval castle in 1929**. Its headquarters, commanding officer's house and two granaries are now
  **marked out in stone on the ground beside the Castle Keep**
  ([Newcastle SiteLines](https://sitelines.newcastle.gov.uk/SMR/204),
  [roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/pons_aelius/)).
- The *Notitia Dignitatum* garrison was **cohors I Cornoviorum**, raised from the Cornovii of
  Cheshire and Shropshire — **the only native British unit known to have been stationed on the
  Wall** ([Co-Curate](https://co-curate.ncl.ac.uk/pons-aelius)).
- Between **1977 and 1992**, **660 burials** were excavated west and north of the keep, from a
  Christian cemetery lying over and largely inside the Roman fort, begun around **AD 700** and in
  use until castle building started in 1168
  ([Newcastle SiteLines](https://sitelines.newcastle.gov.uk/SMR/4359)).
- The **Swing Bridge** the walker crosses under was built by **Sir W. G. Armstrong's Elswick works**
  and opened to river traffic on **17 July 1876** — at the time **the largest swing bridge ever
  built** — and stands on the fourth bridge crossing at this spot
  ([National Transport Trust](https://www.nationaltransporttrust.org.uk/heritage-sites/heritage-detail/newcastle-swing-bridge)).
- **The altars are a mile off the Path**, in the **Great North Museum: Hancock** (free entry,
  managed by North East Museums on behalf of Newcastle University), whose Hadrian's Wall gallery
  holds a scale model of the Wall running the full length of the room
  ([North East Museums](https://www.northeastmuseums.org.uk/greatnorthmuseum/whats-on/hadrians-wall)).

### 3. Newburn Riverside — 19 km

*Town · battlefield · river*

- On **28 August 1640** a Scottish Covenanter army of around **20,000** under **General Alexander
  Leslie** forded the Tyne here against roughly **4,500** English under **Edward, Lord Conway**,
  whose two artillery sconces on the south bank were badly sited and were shot to pieces from the
  rising ground opposite ([Battlefields Trust](https://www.battlefieldstrust.com/resource-centre/battleview.asp?BattleFieldId=29)).
  Historic England maintains a Battlefield Report for the site
  ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/content/docs/listing/battlefields/newburn-ford/)).
- **The consequence is out of all proportion to the fight.** The Scots took Newcastle and with it a
  stranglehold on London's coal. To buy them off, **Charles I had to summon the Long Parliament** —
  the sequence that produced the Civil War two years later
  ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Newburn)).
- The Path here is riverside, not Roman — the National Trail's own description of this section
  offers Tyneside's industrial history and **George Stephenson's birthplace** rather than Wall
  ([National Trails route description](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/route/)).
- **George Stephenson was born in 1781 at High Street House, Wylam**, a stone cottage built c. 1760
  for mining families and then divided among **four families** — the whole Stephenson family lived
  in one room. It is a National Trust property
  ([National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/north-east/george-stephensons-birthplace)).
  See Open questions: I could not confirm from an authoritative source exactly how close the Trail
  passes.

### 4. Heddon-on-the-Wall — 24 km

*Village · Wall remains · English Heritage, free*

- **The first real Wall a west-bound walker meets** — and it is the broad, original specification:
  a consolidated stretch English Heritage puts at **about 220 m** and Historic England at **255 m**,
  standing to about **1.4–1.7 m**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/heddon-on-the-wall-hadrians-wall/history/description/),
  [NHLE 1010616](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1010616)).
- **Get the claim right.** English Heritage says it is *"the longest and best preserved of all those
  so far excavated which were built to broad gauge"* — **not** the longest stretch of Wall anywhere
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/heddon-on-the-wall-hadrians-wall/history/significance/)).
- Broad gauge was a design spec of **10 Roman feet (2.96 m)**, later dropped to 8. When **Parker
  Brewis and F. G. Simpson** measured it in 1926–27 they got **9 ft 7½ in** — the Roman builders
  were working to a target, not a tolerance
  ([Heddon Local History Society, citing *Archaeologia Aeliana* 4th ser. 4 (1927)](https://heddonhistory.weebly.com/hadrians-wall-excavation-1926.html)).
- **Someone cut a corn-drying kiln into the core of the Wall** at the west end of the stretch —
  circular, 1.9 m across, with a paved floor and a flue, late medieval or post-medieval. It was
  exposed *"some date between 1867 and 1879"*
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/heddon-on-the-wall-hadrians-wall/history/description/)).
- The Wall here was **given away in 1924 by the shipping magnate Sir James Knott** to the Society of
  Antiquaries of Newcastle upon Tyne, consolidated 1936–38, and passed into state care
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/heddon-on-the-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Milecastle 12 has never been found**, nor has turret 11a
  ([NHLE 1010617](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1010617)).
- Heddon is where **the Military Road (B6318) begins its dead-straight run west** — the road that
  ate the Wall for the next fifteen miles
  ([Per Lineam Valli](https://perlineamvalli.wordpress.com/2015/06/07/69-what-is-the-military-road/)).

### 5. Halton Chesters (Onnum) and the Portgate — 40 km

*Roman fort · Roman road junction*

- **This is the gate in the Wall.** Dere Street, the great north road from York, crossed Hadrian's
  Wall at **the Portgate** — now under a roundabout on the A68
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/portgate/)).
- **Dorothy Charlesworth's 1966 excavation** found the **west tower of the Portgate in the verge a
  few inches north of the kerb** of the modern road, with foundations projecting some 3 m north of
  the Wall line; John Horsley in **1732** recorded a matching projection south, implying a gatehouse
  roughly 11 m square. **Nothing is visible today**
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/portgate/),
  [Historic England, Charlesworth archive CHA01](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/photos/collection/CHA01)).
- **The knock-on effect is the good detail:** Milecastle 22, 400 m east, had **its north gateway
  blocked with a metre-thick wall shortly after it was built**. The Portgate had made it redundant.
  Excavated by F. G. Simpson in 1930.
- **The names are all post-Roman.** "Portgate" is Old English (*port* + *geat*); the Roman name for
  the gate is unknown. "Dere Street" comes from the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of **Deira** — the Roman
  name for the road is lost too
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/dere-street/)).
- **Halton Chesters (Onnum)**, guarding the crossing 800 m west, was held into the early 5th century
  by the **Ala I Pannoniorum Sabiniana**
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/onnum/)).
- **RIB 1426**, found in a field near the fort in 1848, is a slab reading simply **FVLGVR DIVOM —
  "the lightning of the gods"** — a marker consecrating a spot where lightning struck
  ([RIB 1426](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1426)).
- On the ground west of the roundabout stood **Stagshaw Bank Fair**, one of the greatest livestock
  fairs in England, marked on Armstrong's map of 1769 and eventually banned in the early 20th
  century after persistent disorder
  ([North East History Tour](http://northeasthistorytour.blogspot.com/2017/04/stagshaw-bank-fair-ny982679.html)).

### 6. Chesters Roman Fort (Cilurnum), Chollerford — 49 km

*Roman fort · museum · English Heritage (admission charged)*

- A **cavalry** fort of 2.32 ha built c. AD 124, deliberately straddling the Wall so that three of
  its gates open **north** of the frontier — designed to launch mounted patrols into barbaricum
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/chesters-roman-fort-and-museum-hadrians-wall/history/description/)).
- The garrison from c. AD 178–184 to the end was the **ala II Asturum**, raised in northern Spain.
  The fort needed **16 stable-barracks, each for about 32 men and their horses**; by the 4th century
  only 12 remained in use, roughly 360 horsemen
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/chesters-roman-fort-and-museum-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The regimental **strongroom under the shrine still has its vaulted roof intact** — you go down
  steps into a Roman treasury
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/chesters-roman-fort-and-museum-hadrians-wall/history/description/)).
- The bath house, 70 m east by the North Tyne, is *"the most complete set of Roman baths to be seen
  in Britain"*; its changing hall has **seven arched niches** whose purpose is still argued over
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/chesters-roman-fort-and-museum-hadrians-wall/history/description/)).
- **John Clayton** (1792–1890), Town Clerk of Newcastle, inherited Chesters in 1832, began digging
  in 1843, and bought up stretches of Wall specifically to stop farmers robbing the stone. The site
  museum opened in **1896** and survives as an intact Edwardian display in its original cabinets
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/chesters-roman-fort-and-museum-hadrians-wall/history/significance/)).
- Just east, at **Brunton Turret (26B)**, a 69 m length of Wall shows the moment the specification
  changed mid-build: the broad 3 m gauge visibly narrows to about 1.8 m
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/brunton-turret-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Chesters bridge abutment: two successive bridges, the second (c. AD 160) a full road bridge. Its
  stone was robbed in the **AD 670s** to build St Wilfrid's church at Hexham
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/chesters-bridge-abutment-hadrians-wall/history/)).

### 7. Carrawburgh (Brocolitia): Temple of Mithras and Coventina's Well — 55 km

*Roman fort · temple · English Heritage, free, unstaffed*

- The Mithraeum, built c. AD 200, was **found in the drought summer of 1949 when the tops of three
  altars appeared through the turf**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/temple-of-mithras-carrawburgh-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The three altars name three successive commanding officers: **Lucius Antonius Proculus**, prefect
  of the First Cohort of Batavians (RIB 1544); **Aulus Cluentius Habitus**, of the Ultinian
  voting-tribe, from the colony of Larinum in Italy (RIB 1545); and **Marcus Simplicius Simplex**
  (RIB 1546)
  ([RIB 1544](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1544),
  [1545](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1545),
  [1546](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1546)).
- **The altars standing in the temple today are replicas.** The originals are in the Great North
  Museum: Hancock
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/temple-of-mithras-carrawburgh-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Coventina's Well**, found in October 1876 and dug by John Clayton, was a basin 2.6 × 2.4 m round
  a spring. It held **13,487 coins** — four gold, 184 silver, the rest bronze, running from Mark
  Antony to Gratian — plus ten altars, a relief of three water-nymphs and two clay incense burners
  ([Historic England list entry 1015914](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1015914)).
  English Heritage's own summary rounds this to *"more than 20 altars, about 16,000 coins"*
  ([EH collection](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/temple-of-mithras-carrawburgh-hadrians-wall/collection/)).
- The fort itself sits **on top of the filled-in Vallum** — it was added after the Wall system was
  already laid out
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/temple-of-mithras-carrawburgh-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- 1.5 km east lies **Limestone Corner**, the northernmost point of the Wall, where the ditch was
  abandoned unfinished: undressed blocks still lie where they were left, one estimated at **13
  tons**, another still showing the drilled holes for the iron wedges meant to split it
  ([Heritage Futures](https://heritagefutures.wordpress.com/2019/11/05/hadrians-wall-unfinished-ditch-at-limestone-corner/)).

### 8. Sewingshields Crags and Milecastle 35 — 62 km

*Natural feature · milecastle · English Heritage, free*

- Milecastle 35 is an oddity: its long axis runs **parallel** to the Wall, not perpendicular like
  almost every other. Internally c. 18.3 × 15.2 m, walls up to 3.2 m thick
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/sewingshields-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Excavations in 1978–80 produced **eight spearheads**, shield bosses, jet finger rings, gaming
  boards, six hand-mills and 2nd-century window glass; a stone cist 52 m east held an adult male
  aged 30–40
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/sewingshields-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Turret 35a, near the top of the crags, was **demolished** in the late 2nd or early 3rd century and
  the Wall rebuilt straight over its foundations
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/sewingshields-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Three medieval longhouses**, mid-13th to early 15th century, were later built inside the
  milecastle
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/sewingshields-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Local legend puts **King Arthur and his knights asleep in a cave beneath the crags**, to be woken
  by blowing a bugle and cutting a garter with a stone sword; King's Crags and Queen's Crags lie
  across the moss to the north
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/hadrians-wall-milecastle-35-sewingshields/)).

### 9. Housesteads Roman Fort (Vercovicium) — 64 km

*Roman fort · museum · owned by the National Trust, run by English Heritage (admission charged)*

- 2.2 ha, built within a decade of AD 122. The 3rd-century garrison was **cohors I Tungrorum**, an
  800-strong infantry regiment; also attested are the *cuneus Frisiorum* and the **numerus
  Hnaudifridi** — "Notfried's own unit", a German irregular formation named after its commander
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/housesteads-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The latrines in the south-east corner are *"the best-preserved stone-built latrines, cisterns and
  water channels of any Roman fort in Britain"* — a room about 10 m long seating perhaps **20 men**,
  flushed by a channel fed from a cistern of close to **24,000 litres**. **It still runs when it
  rains**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/housesteads-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history/significance/)).
- The **"Murder House"**: in 1932, under the clay floor of the back room of a shop in the civilian
  settlement, excavators found the skeletons of a middle-aged man and a woman — **the broken tip of
  a sword still lodged in the man's ribs**. Burial inside a settlement was illegal under Roman law,
  so someone hid them there
  ([Undiscovered Scotland](https://www.undiscoveredscotland.co.uk/hexham/housesteads/index.html)).
- A large courtyard building at the centre was probably the **hospital (valetudinarium)**; by the
  4th century it and most of the granaries had been turned into living quarters
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/housesteads-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history/description/)).
- The fort was **given to the National Trust in 1930** and is maintained and staffed by English
  Heritage — a shared arrangement that catches writers out
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/housesteads-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history/significance/)).

### 10. Sycamore Gap and Milecastle 39 (Castle Nick) — 67 km

*Natural feature · milecastle · National Trust land inside the National Park*

**Read §4 before writing a word of this one.**

- The tree was felled with a chainsaw during the night of **27–28 September 2023**. **Daniel Graham**
  (39) and **Adam Carruthers** (32) were convicted at Newcastle Crown Court on **9 May 2025** of two
  counts of criminal damage — one for the tree, one for Hadrian's Wall, which the trunk struck as it
  fell — and jailed for **4 years 3 months each on 15 July 2025**. Damage was valued at **£622,191
  for the tree and £1,144 for the Wall**
  ([CPS](https://cps.gov.uk/north-east/news/pair-who-felled-sycamore-gap-tree-jailed)).
- They drove roughly 30 miles in the dark and **took a wedge of the trunk back to Carlisle as a
  trophy; it has never been recovered**. Mrs Justice Lambert said they acted with "sheer bravado"
  and "revelled in the media coverage"
  ([CPS](https://cps.gov.uk/north-east/news/pair-who-felled-sycamore-gap-tree-jailed)).
- **What is there now:** a living, coppicing stump behind a protective fence. The National Trust
  recorded **25 new shoots** from the base, and shoots have returned for a third consecutive year.
  Visitors are asked not to touch them. The Trust and the National Park will watch for several years
  before deciding whether to keep it as a coppiced stool or reduce it to a single stem
  ([National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/north-east/hadrians-wall-and-housesteads-fort/next-steps-for-the-sycamore-gap-tree)).
- **The largest surviving section of trunk is indoors**, standing upright as it grew, as the
  centrepiece of *Sycamore Gap: Coming Home*, which opened at **The Sill: National Landscape
  Discovery Centre on 11 July 2025**, about two miles from the gap. Artist **Charlie Whinney**
  surrounded it with oak benches from which steam-bent wooden "streams" rise into a canopy shaped
  like a giant sycamore leaf, carrying lines of public-submitted poetry. **Visitors are invited to
  touch it** ([The Sill](https://www.thesill.org.uk/sycamore-gap-coming-home/)).
- **Trees of Hope:** 49 saplings grown from the tree's own seed — **one for each foot of its 49 ft
  height** — gifted to communities across the UK from nearly 500 applications, including the Rob
  Burrow Centre for Motor Neurone Disease in Leeds
  ([The Sill](https://www.thesill.org.uk/sycamore-gap-coming-home/),
  [National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/north-east/hadrians-wall-and-housesteads-fort/trees-of-hope)).
- **Age:** Historic England's laboratory at Fort Cumberland counted an estimated **100–120 growth
  rings** — a minimum, and complicated by the fact that the "tree" was several stems fused together.
  Conclusion: planted in the **late 19th century**, consistent with John Clayton having planted it
  ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/news/new-research-age-sycamore-gap-tree/)).
- **Milecastle 39 (Castle Nick)** sits in the next dip west. Internally c. 19 × 15.5 m, walls to
  c. 1.75 m, re-excavated by **Jim Crow in 1985–87**, which showed the original barrack blocks were
  later replaced by small individual buildings; a stone oven survives in the south-east corner
  ([National Trust HBSMR MNA111212](https://heritagerecords.nationaltrust.org.uk/HBSMR/MonRecord.aspx?uid=MNA111212)).

### 11. Vindolanda — 70 km · **1.5 km OFF-ROUTE, south of the Path**

*Roman fort · museum · the Vindolanda Trust (independent charity, separate ticket)*

- **It is not on Hadrian's Wall and it predates it.** The first fort at Vindolanda *"was built almost
  40 years before the Wall was constructed and was part of an earlier frontier system called the
  Stanegate Frontier"*, along with Magna (Carvoran) and Coria (Corbridge)
  ([Vindolanda Trust FAQ](https://www.vindolanda.com/faqs/why-is-vindolanda-to-the-south-of-hadrians-wall)).
- **Tablet 291.** Around AD 97–105, **Claudia Severa** wrote to **Sulpicia Lepidina**, wife of the
  commanding officer Flavius Cerialis, inviting her to a birthday party on **11 September**: *"for
  the day of the celebration of my birthday, I give you a warm invitation to make sure that you come
  to us, to make the day more enjoyable for me by your arrival."* Most of it is in a scribe's hand;
  the closing lines are Severa's own — *"almost certainly, therefore, these are the earliest known
  examples of writing in Latin by a woman."* It is now in the British Museum, 1986,1001.64
  ([RIB, Tab.Vindol. 291](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/TabVindol291)).
- Waterlogged, oxygen-free soil preserves leather and wood. The 2017 season, in a pre-Hadrianic
  cavalry barrack of c. AD 100, produced **the only known Roman boxing gloves in existence**, two
  rare cavalry swords (one complete in its scabbard), two toy wooden swords, bath clogs, combs and
  dice ([Current Archaeology](https://archaeology.co.uk/articles/news/packing-a-punch-boxing-gloves-found-at-vindolanda.htm),
  [BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-tyne-41225702)).
- The **Vindolanda Trust was established in 1970** to excavate the site for public education and has
  dug it continuously ever since; **over 400 volunteers** join the excavations each year, and the
  Trust funds itself from visitor income
  ([Vindolanda Trust](https://www.vindolanda.com/pages/category/vindolanda-excavations)).
- The first scientific excavations were **Eric Birley's, in the 1930s**
  ([Vindolanda Trust FAQ](https://www.vindolanda.com/faqs/when-was-vindolanda-first-excavated)).

### 12. Cawfields Milecastle 42 and Great Chesters (Aesica) — 73.5 km

*Milecastle · Roman fort · English Heritage, free*

- Milecastle 42 was built by the **Second Legion Augusta** under the governor **Aulus Platorius
  Nepos** (AD 122 – c. 126); one building stone here is dedicated to Hadrian
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/cawfields-roman-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Also found built into it: a stone reused from the **tombstone of Dagvalda, a soldier of the First
  Cohort of Pannonians, set up by his widow Pusinna**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/cawfields-roman-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The gateways survive as monumental blocks with **circular pivot sockets** for double
  inward-opening gates
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/cawfields-roman-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **The dramatic cliff and the flooded pool are a quarry, not a landscape.** The Newcastle Granite &
  Whinstone Company began quarrying whinstone here in **1902**, destroying a long stretch of Wall;
  it closed in **1952** under pressure from preservationists
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/cawfields-roman-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Great Chesters (Aesica)**, just west, holds what is reputed to be **the only Roman altar still
  standing in situ anywhere on the line of the Wall** — walkers habitually leave coins on it
  ([Heritage Futures](https://heritagefutures.wordpress.com/2017/02/24/great-chesters-roman-altar/)).
- The **Aesica brooch** was found here in **1894** in a jewellery hoard in the west tower of the
  south gate, together with an enamelled brooch shaped like a hare, a silver collar and a bronze
  ring set with a Gnostic gem. Its flamboyant trumpet scrollwork makes it a benchmark of
  Romano-British Celtic art, and it gives its name to a whole brooch type
  ([ADS / Northern Studies abstract](https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/catalogue/collections/nsmr03abstracts/6468.html)).
- Between Sycamore Gap and Cawfields the Path crosses **Winshields Crags at 345 m (1,131 ft), the
  highest point on the whole trail**
  ([National Trails route description](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/route/)).

### 13. Walltown Crags, Carvoran/Magna and the Roman Army Museum — 78 km

*Natural feature · museum · Wall in EH care (free); museum run by the Vindolanda Trust (ticketed)*

- At Walltown the Wall survives for **nearly 400 m, standing up to 2.2 m high** — one of the finest
  upstanding stretches anywhere on the frontier
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/walltown-crags-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Turret 45a is older than the Wall.** The Wall meets it at odd angles with vertical straight
  joints, indicating it began as a free-standing Stanegate signal tower and was later swallowed by
  the frontier
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/walltown-crags-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The surviving stretch is **truncated at both ends by Greenhead/Walltown Quarry**, worked for
  roadstone for about a century and closed in **1976**. Destruction was halted in **1943**, when a
  preservation area was defined and the quarry owners compensated
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/walltown-crags-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Carvoran (Magnis/Magna)** sits about 250 yards *south* of the Wall, on the Stanegate at its
  junction with the Maiden Way, and has no link to any milecastle or turret. Its garrison was the
  **cohors I Hamiorum sagittariorum — 500 Syrian archers from Hama, the only regiment of archers
  known to have served in Britain**, attested by diplomas of AD 122, 124, 127 and 135
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/magnis_carvetiorum/)).
- **The Carvoran modius**, a bronze grain measure, was found in a field here in **June 1915 by
  M. Reay, a postman from Greenhead**. Its inscription dates it to c. AD 90 and declares a capacity
  of 17½ *sextarii* — but it actually holds about **20.8**, roughly three too many, which raises the
  question of whether the army was systematically over-measuring the grain tax it took from Britons
  ([RIB instrumentum 2415](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/instrumentum/2415)).
- The **Roman Army Museum is run by the Vindolanda Trust**, which bought Carvoran in **1971**
  ([Vindolanda Trust](https://www.vindolanda.com/roman-army-museum)). The Trust is running a
  five-year Heritage Fund excavation at Magna to 2027; 2025 produced a hoard of 32 leather shoes
  from the defensive ditches, a quarter of them over 30 cm long including a **32.6 cm sole**, and a
  terracotta head of a woman with a four-strand plait found on **5 June 2025**
  ([Roman Army Museum](https://romanarmymuseum.com/about/rare-roman-head/),
  [HeritageDaily](https://www.heritagedaily.com/2025/07/cache-of-roman-footwear-unearthed-at-magna-roman-fort/155547)).

### 14. Thirlwall Castle and Poltross Burn Milecastle (48) — 81 km

*Castle · milecastle · both free; castle leased by Northumberland National Park Authority*

- **Thirlwall Castle is built out of Hadrian's Wall.** Its fabric is *"neatly dressed sandstone
  blocks which had been quarried by the Romans to build Hadrian's Wall"*, a few hundred metres away.
  **John Thirlwall** built or strengthened it in the **1330s**; it was sold to the Earl of Carlisle
  in **1748 for £4,000** and left to fall down
  ([Northumberland National Park](https://www.northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk/discover-explore/places-to-visit/hadrians-wall/thirlwall-castle/)).
  Grade I listed and a scheduled monument
  ([NHLE 1302433](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1302433)).
- Local legend puts a **"Black Dwarf" guarding a golden table down the castle well**, recoverable
  only by the only son of a widow
  ([Northumberland National Park](https://www.northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk/discover-explore/places-to-visit/hadrians-wall/thirlwall-castle/)).
- **Poltross Burn Milecastle (48)** contains the single best physical evidence for how tall the Wall
  system stood: a **surviving flight of stone steps** in the north-east corner whose angle shows the
  wall-walk was **about 3.66 m above ground**, giving an external milecastle-wall height of about
  **5.34 m**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/poltross-burn-milecastle-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- It is larger than a standard milecastle, garrison estimated at **30 soldiers**, with a round oven
  in the north-west corner **rebuilt five successive times**. By the early 19th century it was known
  locally as **"the King's Stables"**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/poltross-burn-milecastle-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Finds from the 1886, 1909–10 and 1965–6 digs include a **jasper intaglio** ring-stone, a
  sword-scabbard chape, scale armour and horse harness
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/poltross-burn-milecastle-hadrians-wall/history/)).

### 15. Birdoswald Roman Fort (Banna) and Willowford Bridge — 85 km

*Roman fort · museum · English Heritage (admission charged)*

- **Banna**, 2.14 ha, begun shortly after AD 122. Garrison: **cohors I Aelia Dacorum**, a
  1,000-strong unit recruited in what is now Romania, whose inscriptions carry the **Dacian falx —
  a curved sword — as a unit badge**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/birdoswald-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history-and-stories/history/)).
- **The headline fact: Rome ends here slowly, not suddenly.** When the granaries went out of use,
  **timber halls were built over the north granary**, in two successive phases, with occupation
  running unbroken into the 5th century. Tony Wilmott reads this as the fort becoming the power base
  of a **local warband descended from the late Roman garrison**, drawing legitimacy from its
  ancestors
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/birdoswald-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history-and-stories/history/)).
- Dated building work: **the granary in AD 205–208, the east gate in AD 219** under governor Modius
  Julius and tribune Marcus Claudius Menander
  ([RIB 1914](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1914)).
- This is the **only place on the entire Wall where the stone Wall diverges from the earlier Turf
  Wall line** — it swings to meet the north corners of the stone fort. Turf Wall turret 49a was
  found buried **under** the fort in 1945, proving the sequence
  ([EH, Harrow's Scar](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/harrows-scar-milecastle-and-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The farmhouse visitors see is a Victorian fake-medieval rebuild by **Henry Norman**, who bought the
  estate in the 1840s, dug the granaries while laying out his garden, and **used the granary
  foundation as a ha-ha**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/birdoswald-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history-and-stories/history/)).
- **Willowford**, just east, is the abutment of the bridge that carried the Wall over the Irthing —
  **now standing high and dry in a field because the river has moved west.** William Camden recorded
  in **1599** that the Irthing then ran further east than it does now. The first stone bridge of
  c. AD 122 had its abutment and easternmost pier **swept away by a flood**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/willowford-wall-turrets-and-bridge-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Walkers cross the Irthing on a **steel-and-timber footbridge built in 1999 and flown into place by
  helicopter** to avoid disturbing the archaeology — the first time the river had been bridged there
  since the Romans
  ([Britain Express](https://www.britainexpress.com/attractions.htm?attraction=3550) — secondary
  source, see Open questions).

### 16. Banks East Turret (52A) and Pike Hill Signal Tower — 89 km

*Turret · signal tower · English Heritage, free, roadside*

- **Pike Hill's tower is older than the Wall.** It was one of a series of signal towers on high
  ground north of the Stanegate, signalling to Nether Denton 1¼ miles off and Castle Hill, Boothby
  2½ miles off. **The Wall was later attached to it at an angle of about 45 degrees** — the tower's
  alignment, not the Wall's, and the visible proof it came first
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/pike-hill-signal-tower-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **It was destroyed in 1870 when a cutting was made for the road beside it.** Parts of two sides
  and one corner survive
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/pike-hill-signal-tower-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Banks East Turret (52A)** is the best-preserved turret in the western sector, and an anomaly:
  **it was built of stone from the outset even though the Wall it served was turf**
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/banks-east-turret-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- Frank Simpson's **1933** excavation found **a mason's hammer, a coin of Hadrian**, several hearths,
  and the **stone platform that was the base for the ladder** to the upper floor
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/banks-east-turret-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **This was the first section of Hadrian's Wall ever taken into state guardianship**, and the road
  was subsequently diverted around it
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/banks-east-turret-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Lanercost Priory** lies about 2 km south — founded **1169 by Robert de Vaux** for Augustinian
  canons *"close to Hadrian's Wall – an excellent source of building stone"*, with Roman inscribed
  stones built into its fabric, **one centurial stone set in upside down**. **Edward I lay there
  from September 1306 to March 1307**, having arrived from Newcastle carried on a litter because he
  could not ride, with a retinue of around 200
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/lanercost-priory/history/),
  [National Churches Trust](https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/explore/story/hadrians-wall)).

### 17. Walton and the Turf Wall — 95 km

*Village · earthwork*

- West of the Irthing the Wall was **first built in turf, not stone** — from Harrow's Scar
  (Milecastle 49) all the way to Bowness. The Turf Wall was **about 6 m wide at the base and
  probably 3.66 m high**, turf-faced over an earth and clay core, cut from the ground beside it
  ([EH, Harrow's Scar](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/harrows-scar-milecastle-and-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Turf Wall milecastles were turf and timber, but the turrets were stone from the start** — which
  is why Banks East survives and its neighbouring milecastles do not
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/banks-east-turret-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The rebuild in stone came in **two separate phases**: Harrow's Scar to Milecastle 51 before
  Hadrian's death in AD 138; the rest only after the Antonine Wall was abandoned and Hadrian's Wall
  reoccupied
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/harrows-scar-milecastle-and-wall-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- **Hare Hill**, near Walton, carries the tallest surviving stretch of Wall at about **3 m** — but
  its north face is a **later-19th-century restoration** organised by George Howard, 9th Earl of
  Carlisle, from fallen Roman facing stones collected and reset, including a centurial stone reading
  `> P·P`, "the century of the senior centurion"
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/hare-hill-hadrians-wall/history/)).
- The fort for this stretch, **Castlesteads (Camboglanna)**, is invisible: it was **levelled in 1791**
  when the gardens of Castlesteads House were laid out over it
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/camboglanna/)).

### 18. Carlisle: Stanwix, Tullie and the Castle — 112 km

*City · Roman fort · museum*

- **Carlisle is older than the Wall by fifty years.** The first timber fort under Carlisle Castle is
  dated by dendrochronology on its rampart oaks to the **autumn/winter of AD 72–73**, built by a
  detachment of **Legio IX Hispana**
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/luguvalium/)).
- **Stanwix (Uxelodunum) was the largest fort on the whole Wall** — about 177 × 213 m, just over
  9¼ acres — and is now entirely buried under the suburb of Stanwix
  ([NHLE 1017948](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1017948)).
- Its garrison was the **ala Petriana, 1,000 cavalry — the only thousand-strong cavalry regiment in
  all of Britain.** Its commander outranked every other officer on the frontier
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/uxelodunum/)).
- **The bath house under the cricket club.** Found in **2017** during evaluation for a new pavilion;
  the first finds were a hypocaust and a stone dedicated to **Julia Domna**, wife of Septimius
  Severus. Community excavation began with a 28-day dig from **31 August 2021** with 150+ volunteers
  on a £99,000 National Lottery Heritage Fund grant
  ([Tullie](https://tullie.org.uk/who-we-are/partnership-projects/uncovering-roman-carlisle/)).
- The finds are extraordinary: **hypocaust tiles stamped IMP**, an imperial mark; two monumental
  carved sandstone heads found in **2023 by a first-time volunteer**, from figures 12–15 ft tall;
  **eighty carved intaglios lost down the bath drains — the largest such group from any single
  context in the UK**, implying Carlisle had its own school of gem engravers; over 20,000 items,
  900+ Roman coins, 300+ hairpins, and Tyrian purple dye. The building is *"the largest on Hadrian's
  Wall so far, 50 m × 60 m and growing"*
  ([Uncovering Roman Carlisle](https://www.uncoveringromancarlisle.co.uk/learnmore)).
  **The dig is still live** — a season ran in May 2026 and produced a 1,800-year-old bronze phallic
  charm
  ([HeritageDaily](https://www.heritagedaily.com/2026/05/roman-penis-charm-unearthed-during-dig-at-carlisle-cricket-club/158000)).
- The **Carlisle writing tablets** are contemporaries of Vindolanda's. One stylus tablet from
  Annetwell Street is a **note of hand — an acknowledgement of debt, the first such document known
  from Britain**
  ([RIB](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/Brit.23.p146)).
- **Carlisle Castle** held **Mary, Queen of Scots in 1568** after she fled Scotland; its dungeon has
  the **"licking stones"**, worn where Jacobite prisoners of 1745 licked moisture from the walls
  ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/carlisle-castle/history/mary-queen-of-scots/)).

### 19. Burgh by Sands (Aballava) — 122 km

*Village · Roman fort · monument*

- **Edward I died here on 7 July 1307**, aged about 68, of dysentery, in camp **on Burgh Marsh**
  while waiting to cross the sands to fight Robert the Bruce
  ([Solway Coast National Landscape](https://www.solwaycoast-nl.org.uk/places-to-explore/historic-sites/king-edward-i-monument/)).
- **His body lay in state in St Michael's Church** before being carried south to Westminster Abbey
  ([Britain Express](https://www.britainexpress.com/attractions.htm?attraction=2308)).
- **St Michael's is built inside the Roman fort, out of the Roman fort** — red and calciferous
  sandstone from the Wall and the fort, the tower of large Roman blocks. It is one of only three
  fortified church towers surviving in Cumbria and the best preserved: the 14th-century west tower
  is a **pele**, entered through a narrow doorway defended by an **iron yett**
  ([NHLE 1367134](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1367134),
  [National Churches Trust](https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-michael-burgh-sands)).
- **The African garrison.** RIB 2042, an altar to Jupiter Optimus Maximus found in **1934 in a
  cottage at Beaumont**, was set up by the **numerus Maurorum Aurelianorum** — a unit of Moors
  raised in Mauretania — styled *Valeriani Gallienique*, which dates it to **AD 253–8**, under
  tribune Flavius Vibianus, with **Julius Rufinus, princeps**, in charge of the work
  ([RIB 2042](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/2042)). The *Notitia Dignitatum*
  still lists the unit at Aballava a century later. It is the basis for the claim that this is the
  **earliest recorded African community in Britain**
  ([Cumbria County History Trust](https://www.cumbriacountyhistory.org.uk/first-recorded-african-community-britain-background-burgh-sands)).
- The **monument on the marsh dates from 1685**, rebuilt in 1803 after it collapsed, restored 1876,
  and stabilised in 2000 to stop it sinking. It stands **1¼ miles NNW of the village**. A separate
  **bronze statue of Edward I was unveiled on the village green in 2007**
  ([Solway Coast National Landscape](https://www.solwaycoast-nl.org.uk/places-to-explore/historic-sites/king-edward-i-monument/),
  [NHLE 1087463](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1087463)).

### 20. Bowness-on-Solway (Maia) — 135 km · **western terminus**

*Terminus · Roman fort · village*

- **Maia was the second largest fort on the Wall**, about 7½ acres, guarding the **last fording
  point of the Solway estuary**. The modern village sits inside its footprint
  ([NHLE 1014702](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1014702)). The name is
  generally taken to mean **"the larger"** — it was the biggest fort west of Stanwix
  ([roman-britain.co.uk](https://www.roman-britain.co.uk/places/bowness/)).
- **St Michael's Church is built very largely of stone from the fort**, the Roman blocks most
  visible in the west wall
  ([National Churches Trust](https://www.nationalchurchestrust.org/church/st-michael-bowness-solway)).
- **The bells.** The church's bells, cast in 1611 and 1616, were **stolen in a Scottish raid in
  1626**; the raiders dropped them in the Solway making for Annan. Bowness men raided back and
  returned with the bells of **Dornock and Middlebie**, which sit in the church porch to this day —
  and **every new minister of Annan is still expected to ask formally for their return**
  ([Visit Cumbria](https://www.visitcumbria.com/car/bowness-on-solway/)).
- **The Banks** is an Edwardian promenade with a shelter looking over the firth. **In 2002 it was
  officially designated the end of the Hadrian's Wall Path**, renovated largely by village
  volunteers, with a Roman-style **mosaic designed from drawings by children of Bowness on Solway
  Primary School**. The shelter holds the final trail passport stamp
  ([Outdooractive / Solway Coast](https://www.outdooractive.com/en/poi/solway-coast/passport-stamp-location-the-banks-promenade-/45034689/)).
- Out in the firth ran the **Solway Junction Railway viaduct** — 1,940 yards, designed by Sir James
  Brunlees, opened **1869** to carry Cumberland iron ore to Scottish steelworks. **Wrecked by ice
  floes in early 1881**, reopened 1884, closed 1921, demolished 1934–35. In its derelict years
  **Scots walked across it to drink in Bowness on Sundays**, Scotland then being dry on the Sabbath
  ([Cumbria Railways](http://www.cumbria-railways.co.uk/solway_viaduct.html),
  [Solway Coast National Landscape](https://www.solwaycoast-nl.org.uk/towns-and-villages/bowness-on-solway/)).
- **The Trail itself opened in May 2003**, the 15th National Trail, managed by Northumberland
  National Park Authority for the Trail Partnership and funded by Natural England
  ([National Trails](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/),
  [Northumberland National Park](https://www.northumberlandnationalpark.org.uk/hwpath20/20-facts-about-hwpnt/)).

## 4. Factual traps

Things a competent writer working from general knowledge will get wrong. Grouped by how badly they
would embarrass us.

### 4.1 Sycamore Gap — the one that will get us in the papers

| Trap | The truth |
| --- | --- |
| Describing a standing tree | **Felled 27–28 September 2023.** Never write it as present. ([CPS](https://cps.gov.uk/north-east/news/pair-who-felled-sycamore-gap-tree-jailed)) |
| "The stump is all that's left" / "the site is bare" | The stump is **alive and coppicing** — 25 shoots recorded by the National Trust, returning for a third year, behind a protective fence. ([National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/north-east/hadrians-wall-and-housesteads-fort/next-steps-for-the-sycamore-gap-tree)) |
| "300 years old" / "stood for centuries" | Historic England's ring count gives **100–120 rings minimum, planted late 19th century**, and the tree was several fused stems. Write "well over a century". ([Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/whats-new/news/new-research-age-sycamore-gap-tree/)) |
| "The trunk lies where it fell" | The **largest section stands indoors at The Sill**, opened 11 July 2025 as *Sycamore Gap: Coming Home*. ([The Sill](https://www.thesill.org.uk/sycamore-gap-coming-home/)) |
| "A replacement tree has been planted" | **No replacement has been planted at the gap.** The 49 *Trees of Hope* saplings were gifted to communities **elsewhere in the UK**. ([National Trust](https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/north-east/hadrians-wall-and-housesteads-fort/trees-of-hope)) |
| Confusing "Tree of Hope" with "Trees of Hope" | The 49 saplings are the **Trees of Hope**. The installation at The Sill is **Sycamore Gap: Coming Home** by artist Charlie Whinney. Two different things. |
| Vagueness about the crime | Daniel Graham (39) and Adam Carruthers (32), **convicted 9 May 2025**, **jailed 4 years 3 months each on 15 July 2025**, Newcastle Crown Court; damage valued at **£622,191 (tree) and £1,144 (Wall)**. ([CPS](https://cps.gov.uk/north-east/news/pair-who-felled-sycamore-gap-tree-jailed)) |
| "The Robin Hood tree, from the film" | True as a nickname, but the nickname came *from* the 1991 film, not from local tradition — and the film's geography is nonsense: Robin lands at Dover and reaches Nottingham via Hadrian's Wall. |

**Recommended framing for copy:** the gap, not the tree. The dip in the crags, the fence, the green
shoots, the trunk two miles away at The Sill that visitors are invited to touch. That is a better
piece of writing than the tree ever was, and it is accurate.

### 4.2 Vindolanda — off-route, and older than the Wall

**Confirmed, both parts.**

- **Off-route.** Vindolanda is roughly **1.5 km south of the Path**, reached by a marked detour near
  Once Brewed / The Sill; walkers report 25–30 minutes each way. It is not on the Wall and cannot be
  seen from it.
- **Predates the Wall.** The Vindolanda Trust states the first fort *"was built almost 40 years
  before the Wall was constructed and was part of an earlier frontier system called the Stanegate
  Frontier"*
  ([Vindolanda Trust](https://www.vindolanda.com/faqs/why-is-vindolanda-to-the-south-of-hadrians-wall)).
  The Stanegate is a *road* frontier — forts strung along a road, with no continuous barrier.
- **Different owner, different ticket.** Vindolanda and the Roman Army Museum belong to the
  **Vindolanda Trust**, an independent charity founded in 1970. Chesters, Housesteads, Birdoswald
  and Corbridge are **English Heritage**. English Heritage membership does not get you into
  Vindolanda. ([Vindolanda Trust](https://www.vindolanda.com/pages/category/vindolanda-excavations))

**Should it be in the Journey? Yes — but labelled as a detour.** Three reasons: (1) it is the single
richest source of *human* material on the whole route, and our Landmark writing is about people, not
masonry; (2) essentially every walker who has heard of it goes; (3) the Path's own official
circular-walk offering routes walkers to it
([National Trails](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/circular-linear-walks/)).
The copy should say plainly that the walker turns south off the Wall — which is also the *point* of
Vindolanda, because it is why the site exists.

**Note for the app:** at 70 km it sits 3 km after Sycamore Gap and 3.5 km before Cawfields. If the
product ever wants strict minimum spacing, Vindolanda is the entry to move, not to delete.

### 4.3 The Wall itself — the standing misconceptions

- **It is not the England–Scotland border and never has been.** *"It's sometimes mistakenly thought
  that the Wall marks the English/Scottish border. In fact, Northumberland, England's largest
  county, lies mainly north of Hadrian's Wall"*
  ([Hadrian's Wall Country](https://hadrianswallcountry.co.uk/explore/the-history-of-the-wall/)).
  The Wall lies wholly within England. The English and the Scots did not exist as peoples for
  centuries after it was built.
- **"Defensive rampart" is the wrong frame.** The same source: *"a function, probably the chief
  function, of the Wall, was frontier control, just like modern frontier barriers"*, and notes the
  Wall is not well sited for defence. The milecastles are **gates** — roughly one every Roman mile —
  which is not how you build something to stop an army; it is how you build something to tax, count
  and channel people. English Heritage does still describe it as marking *and protecting* the
  frontier ([English Heritage FAQ](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/faqs-hadrians-wall/)), so do
  not overcorrect into "it was purely a customs post" — the honest line is **a controlled frontier,
  garrisoned, with defence one function among several.**
- **The Wall is 73 miles; the Path is 84.** English Heritage: *"73 miles long (or 80 Roman miles)"*
  ([EH FAQ](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/faqs-hadrians-wall/)). The extra 11 miles is mostly
  the riverside detour through Tyneside. Do not write "the 84-mile Wall".
- **It was not built by slaves.** *"Roman soldiers from the three legions of the province"* built it
  ([EH FAQ](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/faqs-hadrians-wall/)).
- **It was probably never "finished".** The abandoned ditch and part-split boulders at Limestone
  Corner are read by Breeze and Graafstal as evidence the Wall was still incomplete at Hadrian's
  death in AD 138
  ([The Past](https://the-past.com/feature/an-unfinished-frontier-was-hadrians-wall-completed-in-his-lifetime/)).
- **Hadrian's Wall was not the Empire's northernmost frontier for long.** The Antonine Wall replaced
  it around AD 143; Hadrian's Wall was reoccupied around AD 160
  ([EH FAQ](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/faqs-hadrians-wall/)).

### 4.4 Sites where the popular story is wrong, or has changed

| Site | Trap |
| --- | --- |
| **Housesteads** | **Owned by the National Trust** (given 1930), **operated by English Heritage.** Attributing it wholly to either is the standard error. ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/housesteads-roman-fort-hadrians-wall/history/significance/)) |
| **Housesteads latrines** | The sponge-on-a-stick image is extrapolated from Mediterranean practice. Sponges are not native to British waters and there is no direct British evidence. Hedge it. |
| **Milecastle 37's arch** | Only the **two lowest springers** are Roman; the upper stones were re-set in modern times for effect. 19th- and 20th-century drawings show them absent. Do not call it a surviving Roman arch. |
| **Carrawburgh Mithraeum** | **The altars on site are replicas**; the originals are in the Great North Museum: Hancock. And the *fort* is an unexcavated grass platform — not a ruin. ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/temple-of-mithras-carrawburgh-hadrians-wall/history/)) |
| **Limestone Corner** | Isn't limestone at the point that matters — the ditch was cut into hard Whin Sill dolerite. And "the rock defeated them" is incomplete: the Vallum *was* cut through comparable rock nearby. |
| **Cawfields and Walltown cliffs** | **Man-made quarries**, not natural crags. Cawfields' flooded "lough" is a whinstone pit worked 1902–52; Walltown's is Greenhead Quarry, closed 1976. Both destroyed long stretches of Wall. ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/cawfields-roman-wall-hadrians-wall/history/), [EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/walltown-crags-hadrians-wall/history/)) |
| **The Carvoran modius** | Found at Carvoran, but **displayed at Chesters** in the Clayton Collection — English Heritage's care, not the Vindolanda Trust's museum next door. |
| **Carvoran naming** | The Vindolanda Trust now brands it **"Magna Roman Fort"**; OS maps and older literature say **Carvoran**; the Roman name is usually **Magnis**. Same place. |
| **Great Chesters aqueduct** | Scheduled as an aqueduct and traditionally described as a 6-mile channel dropping 20 m, but **never properly excavated**, and at least one field critique argues the traced earthwork would have to run uphill. Attribute, don't assert. |
| **Hare Hill** | Routinely captioned "the tallest surviving stretch of Hadrian's Wall" without saying the **north face is a later-19th-century rebuild** for the 9th Earl of Carlisle. ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/hare-hill-hadrians-wall/history/)) |
| **Castlesteads (Camboglanna)** | Often listed as a fort near Walton to visit. It was **levelled in 1791** for a garden. Nothing is there. |
| **Birdoswald's post-Roman halls** | The stratigraphy is solid; the *c.* AD 520 abandonment and the "warband descended from the garrison" reading are **Tony Wilmott's model**, debated. Attribute it. |
| **Birdoswald's "watchtower"** | Ian Richmond misidentified it in the 1930s. It was **shown to be a bathhouse in 2021**. Old guidebooks still repeat the error. |
| **Which digs are live** | **Birdoswald's excavations ended with the 2024 season. Carlisle's cricket-club dig is still running** (a season ran May 2026). Easy to state backwards. ([Newcastle University](https://www.ncl.ac.uk/press/articles/archive/2024/06/birdoswaldfinalexcavations), [Uncovering Roman Carlisle](https://www.uncoveringromancarlisle.co.uk/learnmore)) |
| **"Tullie House Museum"** | Renamed simply **"Tullie" in July 2024**, and **reopened in May 2025** after a major revamp. It has been an independent charitable trust since 2011. ([Museums Association](https://www.museumsassociation.org/museums-journal/news/2025/05/tullie-museum-and-art-gallery-welcomes-visitors-back-after-revamp/)) |
| **Carlisle tablets** | Not "Britain's oldest handwriting" — that is the **Bloomberg tablets from London**. Carlisle's real claim is the **first note of hand (debt acknowledgement) known from Britain**. |
| **The Carlisle carved heads** | Being **Septimius Severus and Julia Domna is a hypothesis.** The Julia Domna dedication stone and the IMP tile stamps are the hard evidence. |
| **"Petriana"** | The fort at Stanwix was probably **Uxelodunum**; "Petriana" looks like a Roman clerical confusion of the fort's name with its regiment's. Write *Uxelodunum (Petriana)*. |
| **Stanwix vs Bowness** | **Stanwix was the largest fort on the Wall; Bowness/Maia was second.** The Solway Coast National Landscape's own page calls Maia "the largest on the wall" — an authoritative-looking source that is wrong. |
| **Poltross Burn's steps** | They give the height of the **milecastle wall** (3.66 m wall-walk, 5.34 m external), not of Hadrian's Wall itself. Extending the figure is an inference. ([EH](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/poltross-burn-milecastle-hadrians-wall/history/)) |
| **Poltross Burn's nickname** | English Heritage records **"the King's Stables"**. "King Arthur's Stables" is a later folk gloss. |
| **Thirlwall Castle's date** | Genuinely split. Some sources say 12th century, later strengthened; Northumberland National Park says **John Thirlwall, 1330s**. Safest: a 14th-century tower house on an earlier site. |
| **Edward I at Burgh by Sands** | He died **in camp on the marsh**, 1¼ miles from the village, not in the village. The **monument is from 1685** (rebuilt 1803); the **bronze statue on the green is from 2007**. Don't conflate them. |
| **Lanercost Priory** | Half of it is a **live parish church** (St Mary Magdalene), not a ruin. And Edward I was there **September 1306 – March 1307**, five months, not "in 1307". |
| **Drumburgh Castle** | **A private house.** No public access. Do not imply visitability. |
| **Segedunum's bath house** | The full-scale reconstructed bath house has been **closed for essential maintenance**. Check before writing "you can walk into a Roman bath house". ([Visit Hadrian's Wall](https://www.visithadrianswall.co.uk/things-to-do/segedunum-roman-fort-baths-and-museum-p715271/)) |
| **The eastern Wall in Newcastle** | **The National Trail does not follow the Wall through Newcastle.** It takes the riverside; the Wall's line runs along the West Road, well to the north. Denton Hall Turret, Benwell and the Temple of Antenociticus are **not on the Path**. ([North East Museums](https://www.northeastmuseums.org.uk/walking-hadrian-s-wall-on-tyneside)) |
| **"General Wade's Military Road"** | **A misnomer, and near-universal in travel writing.** Field Marshal Wade **died in 1748**, three years before construction began. The road was authorised by an Act of 1751, surveyed in 1749 by **Dugal Campbell and Hugh Debbeig**, and built by civil contractors 1751–57. ([Per Lineam Valli](https://perlineamvalli.wordpress.com/2015/06/07/69-what-is-the-military-road/)) |
| **Segedunum and the Wall's east end** | **The Wall's first eastern terminus was Pons Aelius, not Wallsend.** Segedunum and the four-mile extension came later (c. AD 125–127) and were built to a narrower gauge. "The Wall ran Wallsend to Bowness from the start" is wrong. ([North East Museums](https://www.northeastmuseums.org.uk/segedunum/about-us)) |
| **Heddon-on-the-Wall** | **Not "the longest continuous stretch of Hadrian's Wall."** EH's actual claim is the longest and best preserved excavated stretch **built to broad gauge**. EH and Historic England also disagree on its length (220 m vs 255 m) and height — hedge or attribute. |
| **The Heddon kiln** | A **corn-drying** kiln, at the **west** end, not a lime kiln. EH dates it only "late medieval or post-medieval". A circulating claim that John Clayton found it c. 1883 conflicts with EH's "exposed some date between 1867 and 1879" — Clayton is the default name things get attached to on this Wall. |
| **The Errington Arms, Portgate** | **No longer a pub.** CAMRA records it closed as a pub on 01/11/2018 and reclassified as a restaurant/café. Do not send walkers there for a pint. ([CAMRA](https://camra.org.uk/pubs/errington-arms-stagshaw-bank-193524)) |
| **Rudchester** | The Mithraeum was **backfilled after the 1953 excavation — nothing is visible.** The fort field was **sold by Northumberland County Council on 28 September 2012**; sources still describe it as council-owned. And the garrison (*cohors I Frisiavonum*) is a documentary inference — **no stone from the site names a unit**. |
| **Rudchester RIB 1398** | Reads only ***DEO*** — "to the God". **Mithras is not named.** Writers routinely quote it as "to Mithras, Lucius Sentius Castus…", which is an interpolation. ([RIB 1398](https://romaninscriptionsofbritain.org/inscriptions/1398)) |
| **The Portgate** | **Nothing is visible** — lost to road widening and tree planting. The "36 ft square" gatehouse is half-inferred from Horsley (1732), not excavated. |
| **Museum attributions** | Everything from Benwell, Rudchester, Carrawburgh and Newcastle moved: Black Gate → Museum of Antiquities → **Great North Museum: Hancock (2009)**. Older books name the wrong museum. |
| **Whittle Dene** | An inverted-causation trap. The reservoirs opened in **1848, five years before** the 1853 cholera epidemic, and the 1854 Royal Commission found the water company was **pumping Tyne water from below the town's sewer outfalls**. They are the scandal, not the cure. |

## 5. Spacing: where the dead stretches are, and what to do about them

**The real distribution is lumpy, and we should not pretend otherwise.** The central sector is the
dramatic part; the eastern end is urban and the western end is saltmarsh.

### The two dead stretches

1. **km 24 → 40 (16 km), Heddon-on-the-Wall to Halton Chesters.** The Military Road was driven along
   the Wall's foundations here in 1751–57 (**not** by General Wade, who died in 1748 — see §4.4).
   There is almost nothing upstanding for 15 miles, and the Path walks field edges beside a B-road.
   The official route description is candid:
   *"With not much in the way of gradients this shouldn't be too taxing a section along mostly the
   edges of arable farmland"*
   ([National Trails](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/route/)).
2. **km 95 → 112 (17 km), Walton to Carlisle.** Pasture, buried Wall, and the Eden valley. Official
   description: *"By now the lack of masonry won't concern you. Spotting the humps and bumps in the
   ground; Vallum here, Wall ditch there, the buried Wall even, will be second nature."*

At 5 km/day these are 3 and 3½ days without an arrival. That is the worst the Journey gets.

### Optional fillers — real places, if we decide the gaps are too long

I have **not** invented anything. These are genuine, sourceable sites in the gaps, listed so the
decision is ours rather than forced:

| Approx. km | Candidate | Why it is second-tier |
| --: | --- | --- |
| 26 | Rudchester (Vindobala) Roman fort and Mithraeum | Only 2 km after Heddon — worsens clustering rather than fixing the gap |
| 35 | Whittle Dene / the Robin Hood Inn | It is a pub, but it is **one of only seven official Trail Passport stamping stations**, confirmed open and trading, with the Wall ditch surviving immediately west of it and the Vallum as an upstanding earthwork opposite ([CAMRA](https://camra.org.uk/pubs/robin-hood-inn-east-wallhouses-193785), [Per Lineam Valli](https://perlineamvalli.wordpress.com/2016/01/24/driving-hadrians-wall-the-minor-car-parks-xi-east-wall-houses/)) |
| 54 | Limestone Corner | Strong story, but 1.5 km from Carrawburgh — merge, don't add |
| 90 | Lanercost Priory | ~2 km off-route; currently folded into Landmark 16 |
| 102 | Newtown / Bleatarn | Thin; a stretch of Vallum earthwork |
| 130 | Drumburgh Castle (Coggabata) | Genuinely good material, private house, 5 km before the finish — would tighten a good final stretch |

**My recommendation: fill the eastern gap with the Robin Hood Inn (≈35 km) and leave the western
gap alone.** That takes the list to 21 and turns 16 km into 11 + 5. The Walton→Carlisle gap is
better left as a long quiet walk into a city — it is dramatically correct, and Carlisle is a big
arrival.

### The crowded middle

Landmarks 8–16 (62–89 km) sit 2–4.5 km apart. At 5 km/day a user gets an arrival most days through
this stretch. **This is a feature, not a bug** — it is the reward for the eastern slog, and it is
where the actual Wall is. If it ever needs thinning, drop in this order: Sewingshields (8), then
Cawfields/Great Chesters (12), then Banks East/Pike Hill (16).

## 6. Open questions / unverified

Stated plainly rather than guessed.

1. **There is no published official cumulative distance chart for the Hadrian's Wall Path.** The
   National Trail site gives the total and one internal figure. The six-section mileages I used as
   anchors are **uncited on Wikipedia** and I could not trace them to Natural England or to the
   official National Trail Guide. They survive cross-checks (§1) but they are not primary. **If we
   want better numbers, the route is to buy the official National Trail Guide or the Cicerone/
   Trailblazer guide and read the distance chart** — those are print-only and were not fetchable.
2. **Every intermediate Landmark distance is my interpolation, not a sourced figure.** ±1.5–2 km.
   See §1 for the method. Do not present these to users as trail mileages.
3. **Wall-mile positions for a few Landmarks are approximate.** I am confident of Segedunum (0),
   Heddon (12), Chesters (27), Milecastle 39 (39), Milecastle 42 (42), Poltross Burn (48),
   Birdoswald (49), Banks East (52A) and Bowness (80). Halton Chesters/Portgate (≈21.5–22),
   Carrawburgh (≈31), Sewingshields (35), Housesteads (≈36.5), Walltown (≈45), Walton (≈55.5),
   Stanwix (≈65) and Burgh by Sands (≈71) are from general Wall literature and were not each
   confirmed against a National Heritage List entry.
4. **How close the Path passes George Stephenson's Birthplace at Wylam is unconfirmed.** The
   National Trail's own section description names it as a feature of the Wallsend→Heddon stage, but
   I could not source the actual distance from the Trail to the cottage. Wylam is upstream of
   Newburn; treat it as "nearby", not "on the route", until checked.
5. **The Willowford footbridge details are secondary-sourced.** "Built 1999, flown into place by
   helicopter" is consistently reported, but **English Heritage's own page does not mention the
   footbridge at all**. The span, cost, architect and opening date come from
   `britainexpress.com`/`geograph`. Safe to say 1999 and helicopter; check anything more specific.
6. **The Robin Hood Inn's claimed 1752 date is a pub-trade claim.** It is not a listed building, so
   there is no architectural verification. Its status as a passport stamping station *is* sourced.
7. **The King's Arms at Bowness** — I could not verify any specific historical claim (date,
   ownership, its role as the traditional finishing pint) from an authoritative source. Do not use
   specifics.
8. **`historicengland.org.uk` returns HTTP 403 to automated fetching.** All National Heritage List
   content cited here was recovered via search snippets and corroborated on secondary sites. If
   exact HE wording is ever load-bearing in published copy, open the list-entry URL in a browser
   first.
9. **`vindolanda.com` returns HTTP 403 to automated fetching.** The Vindolanda Trust quotations here
   come from search-engine extracts of those exact pages, not from a direct fetch. The quotations
   are verbatim but should be eyeballed before publication.
10. **Coventina's Well coin count is genuinely inconsistent between sources** — 13,487 in the
    excavation record (Historic England) versus "about 16,000" in English Heritage's own collection
    summary. Prefer "around 13,500" if you want the archaeologically precise figure, and do not
    present a single number as settled.
11. **English Heritage and Historic England disagree on Heddon's dimensions** (220 m vs 255 m;
    1.4 m/six courses vs 1.7 m/seven courses; "up to 2 m thick" vs "2.8–3 m"). Hedge.
12. **The Sycamore Gap site changes.** The National Trust has announced a further creative
    commission whose public phase begins **summer 2026**, and the fence around the stump is expected
    to be enlarged as the shoots grow. **This entry needs a re-check before each release.**
13. **Live diversions exist on the Trail** and are not a content problem but are worth knowing
    about: an underpass between Grinsdale and Engine Lonning near Carlisle floods, and a bridge
    repair closed a Carlisle section in August 2026
    ([National Trails news](https://www.nationaltrail.co.uk/en_GB/trails/hadrians-wall-path/news/)).
14. **"Defensive vs frontier-control" is a live scholarly argument, not a settled fact.** Hadrian's
    Wall Country and much current archaeology emphasise control; English Heritage's own FAQ still
    uses "protect". Our copy should reflect the tension rather than pick a side loudly.
